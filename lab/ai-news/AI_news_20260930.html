Executive Summary

September 29, 2026 was anchored by two headline events whose combination reveals a deepening tension in AI governance: OpenAI's DevDay 2026 product launch and a White House summit where the Trump administration and major AI CEOs signed a voluntary "accord" endorsing industry self-regulation over legislative intervention. Taken together, these events surface a recurrent IS governance puzzle — the gap between rapidly expanding agentic AI capabilities and the institutional mechanisms society has in place to oversee them.

OpenAI's DevDay introduced Dots, a suite of persistent, always-on AI agents designed to operate across organizational platforms with "minimal oversight," alongside a restructured developer ecosystem (Agents API, Decisions API, ChatGPT Space, OpenAI Marketplace, "Sign in with ChatGPT"). The DevDay suite signals that agentic AI is transitioning from a prototype to a productized organizational infrastructure layer — raising immediate questions about delegation, accountability, and human-AI task boundaries in enterprise settings. ChatGPT's reported 1.2 billion weekly users underscores the scale at which these governance questions now apply.

The White House summit produced a voluntary accord on AI self-regulation, with Trump calling it "morally binding" and formally renaming "artificial intelligence" to "super intelligence" in executive language. The non-binding nature of the agreement, combined with dissenting voices from Anthropic's CEO and Palantir's CEO who raised safety concerns even at the event, illustrates what might be characterized as a structured ambiguity problem in AI governance: public officials endorsing self-policing while industry insiders signal its insufficiency. A Reuters/Ipsos poll finding that 73% of respondents believe AI companies have fallen short on preventing harm adds empirical texture to public legitimacy concerns.

Separately, Google filed two legal challenges at the EU General Court against Digital Markets Act orders that would compel it to share Android AI capabilities and search data with rivals by 2027. This marks a significant escalation in platform ecosystem governance, with privacy framing deployed as a competitive instrument. The DMA AI conflict is among the clearest observable test cases for theorizing AI-mediated platform competition and regulatory boundary-setting under conditions of technical asymmetry.

Note: All four developments were verified from original sources dated September 29, 2026. Excluded sources lacked verifiable publication dates or were originally published on earlier dates.

Key Takeaways

  • Takeaway 1 — Agentic AI as enterprise infrastructure: OpenAI's DevDay product suite treats persistent AI agents not as assistive tools but as persistent organizational participants with identities, credentials, and cross-platform operating authority — a framing that challenges traditional human-computer interaction models in IS and raises underexplored questions about AI agent accountability in workflow design.
  • Takeaway 2 — Voluntary self-regulation as the dominant US governance model: The White House AI summit produced a non-binding voluntary accord rather than legislative action, with attendees including the CEOs of all major US AI labs. This pattern of government-facilitated self-regulation, publicly framed as morally binding but institutionally unenforceable, appears to be consolidating as the dominant US policy approach — a sociotechnical arrangement that may deserve theoretical treatment as a distinct governance regime type.
  • Takeaway 3 — Privacy rhetoric as competitive strategy in platform governance: Google's EU legal challenge frames DMA compliance as a privacy risk rather than a market-power dispute. When a privacy argument simultaneously serves as a market-access defense, it complicates straightforward readings of AI data governance compliance and warrants research attention to how privacy framing is strategically deployed in platform ecosystem conflicts.
  • Takeaway 4 — Agentic AI product design emphasizes autonomy and de-emphasizes human oversight: Multiple DevDay products — particularly Dots — are explicitly designed to operate with "minimal oversight," representing a vendor-level design philosophy that may not align with organizational risk management practices or emerging governance expectations. The design choice itself is empirically observable and theoretically interesting.
  • Takeaway 5 — AI governance legitimacy gap is becoming measurable: A Reuters/Ipsos poll finding that 73% of respondents believe AI companies have fallen short on preventing harm provides a public legitimacy data point that IS researchers interested in institutional theory and AI governance may wish to monitor longitudinally. The co-occurrence of this finding with the voluntary accord signing is itself a notable juxtaposition.

The OpenAI Marketplace and "Sign in with ChatGPT" suggest a deliberate platform ecosystem strategy — creating AI-mediated dependency relationships between enterprises, developers, and the OpenAI platform — that raises questions about platform competition dynamics, complementor bargaining power, and switching costs analogous to earlier app-store governance debates.

Theory Mapping
Platform Ecosystem Theory / Generativity Theory

Core logic: Platforms create value by enabling third-party complementors to build on shared infrastructure; generativity describes the platform's capacity to produce emergent uses through third-party innovation (Zittrain, 2008; Parker & Van Alstyne, 2005).

Potential fit: The OpenAI Marketplace, Agents API, and Sign in with ChatGPT may be studied as deliberate generativity-expanding moves that simultaneously increase switching costs and information asymmetries between platform and complementors. The agentic layer (Dots) adds a new dimension: AI agents themselves may become complementors or consumers within the ecosystem, a configuration not well theorized in existing platform literature.

Key assumptions: Assumes complementors are rational profit-seeking firms; may not account for AI agents as non-human participants within the ecosystem; boundary conditions around human-only governance may require extension.

Agency Theory

Core logic: Principals delegate tasks to agents whose interests may diverge; information asymmetry makes monitoring costly (Jensen & Meckling, 1976; Eisenhardt, 1989).

Potential fit: When organizations deploy Dots agents to act "with minimal oversight," a novel principal-agent configuration emerges where the AI agent is the immediate executor, the employing organization is an intermediate principal, and OpenAI retains model-level authority. Standard monitoring mechanisms (behavioral vs. outcome control) are complicated because AI agent "behavior" is not directly observable in human terms. This may require extension of traditional agency theory to multi-layer, human-AI principal chains.

Key assumptions: Traditional agency theory assumes human agents with interests and motivations; AI agents lack interests in the traditional sense but exhibit goal-directed behavior that may drift from principal intent; whether "alignment" is the correct substitute construct requires examination.

Sociotechnical Systems Theory / Adaptive Structuration Theory

Core logic: Technologies and social systems co-produce organizational structures through appropriation and adaptation; the design of a technology embeds social assumptions that are reinforced or subverted in use (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Trist, 1981).

Potential fit: The "minimal oversight" design philosophy embedded in Dots could be studied through an AST lens: how do organizations appropriate agentic AI features in ways that either reinforce or resist this design assumption? Longitudinal organizational case studies may reveal adaptation patterns that have not yet crystallized.

Key assumptions: Requires longitudinal field access to organizations deploying Dots agents; current evidence is limited to vendor announcements.

White House AI Summit: Trump and AI CEOs Sign Voluntary "Accord" on Self-Regulation; AI Renamed "Super Intelligence"

Independent Secondary Reporting Axios, The Next Web, QZ · September 29, 2026 · Axios · The Next Web · QZ
AI Governance Self-Regulation Institutional Pressure AI Policy Legitimacy Platform Governance
Evidence Quality
Independent Secondary Reporting Multiple independent news organizations (Axios, The Next Web, QZ) reported on the same event with corroborating factual details including attendance lists and specific quotes. The accord itself is a voluntary non-binding document; its text was not published in full. Attendance list, Trump's statements, and the voluntary/non-binding nature of the accord are reported across multiple outlets and are treated here as factual. The Reuters/Ipsos poll (73% respondents say AI companies fell short) is a survey finding, not a behavioral observation, and is labeled accordingly.
Verified Summary

On September 29, 2026, President Trump hosted approximately 31 AI industry executives at a White House lunch in the East Room, including Elon Musk, Mark Zuckerberg, Jeff Bezos, Satya Nadella, Sundar Pichai, Dario Amodei (Anthropic), Jensen Huang (Nvidia), Lisa Su (AMD), and Greg Brockman representing OpenAI. House Speaker Mike Johnson and multiple Cabinet members attended. The meeting produced a voluntary "accord" on AI governance standards. Trump described the accord as "morally binding," stating the companies would "police each other." The accord text referenced internal risk reviews, external audits, and multiple layers of auditing controls, but the full document was not published.

Trump stated: "There's a belief that there should be tremendous self-regulation, and we automatically have regulation with the Department of Justice, the FBI, all of that." No new legislative or regulatory requirements were announced. Trump signed an executive order designating "super intelligence" as official U.S. government terminology for AI, replacing "artificial intelligence." A Reuters/Ipsos poll, cited in reporting, found that 73% of respondents believed AI companies had "fallen short on preventing serious societal harm" (survey finding, not behavioral observation). Anthropic's Dario Amodei and Palantir's Alex Karp publicly raised safety concerns during the event. The White House also announced America.gov, an AI-driven federal information portal.

The accord's enforcement mechanism, scope, and monitoring procedures were not specified in available reporting.

Why This May Matter

This event may represent a crystallization of the US AI governance regime as a "regulated self-regulation" model — where the federal government convenes industry, publicly endorses commitments, but does not create enforcement mechanisms or legal liability. From an institutional theory perspective, this creates a distinctive configuration: the state provides legitimacy resources (White House imprimatur, presidential framing as "morally binding") without exercising coercive authority. Whether this produces isomorphic pressure on non-participating AI firms, or whether it functions primarily as a legitimacy-management ritual, is an empirically open question.

The co-presence of CEOs who raised safety concerns (Amodei, Karp) with those who favor development acceleration (Musk, Huang) within the same voluntary accord raises questions about the internal heterogeneity of industry self-regulation. If the accord commits firms to audit processes, the presence or absence of meaningful audit activity becomes an observable empirical check on accord compliance — potentially constituting a natural experiment for studying voluntary commitment effectiveness.

The renaming to "super intelligence" is symbolically interesting as a framing device: it signals an executive branch position on the developmental trajectory of AI, which may affect how regulatory agencies, courts, and international counterparts engage with the technology going forward.

Theory Mapping
Institutional Theory (DiMaggio & Powell; Meyer & Rowan)

Core logic: Organizations adopt structures and practices to gain legitimacy within institutional fields; change is driven by coercive, mimetic, and normative pressures toward isomorphism.

Potential fit: The White House accord may be studied as a coercive-adjacent legitimacy event that produces normative pressure on AI firms to adopt safety governance practices regardless of whether legal enforcement follows. The accord's "morally binding" framing invokes normative legitimacy explicitly. Whether this produces substantive behavioral change or ceremonial adoption of governance structures (decoupling) is a classic institutional theory question directly applicable here.

Key assumptions: Assumes organizations respond to institutional pressure; requires examination of whether AI labs are subject to field-level norms or operate in a pre-institutional space where norms are still forming.

Commons Governance Theory (Ostrom)

Core logic: Commons resources can be sustainably governed through collective action institutions with defined rules, monitoring, and graduated sanctions; effective self-governance requires design principles that voluntary agreements often lack.

Potential fit: If AI safety is conceived as a collective action problem — where each firm benefits from others' safety investments but has incentives to under-invest — Ostrom's framework could be used to assess whether the White House accord satisfies the design principles for effective self-governance (clear boundaries, monitoring mechanisms, graduated sanctions). Current evidence suggests the accord lacks formal monitoring and sanctions — a potentially significant gap.

Key assumptions: Requires acceptance of AI safety as a commons problem; the accord's vagueness makes it difficult to evaluate against Ostrom's criteria without further disclosure of its provisions.

Potential Mechanism: Institutional theory predicts that organizations facing legitimacy threats may ceremonially adopt governance structures (decoupling) while maintaining unchanged operational practices. The voluntary accord, with no enforcement mechanism, high public legitimacy expectations, and internal dissent, provides a natural setting to study this tension.

Potential Data Source: Longitudinal analysis of signatory firms' AI safety disclosures, hiring of safety staff, audit reports (if published), and incident rates over 1-3 years post-accord; natural language processing of press releases and earnings calls to track governance language.

Closest Literature to Check: Institutional theory applications to IT governance (Liang et al.); voluntary corporate sustainability commitments literature; AI governance literature (Cihon et al.; Dafoe); self-regulatory organizations literature (economics and law).

Evidence Needed: Whether prior voluntary AI governance commitments (e.g., 2023 voluntary commitments at White House) produced measurable behavioral change; longitudinal data on firm-level AI safety investment post-commitment.

Main Feasibility Risk: Long time horizon to observe behavioral outcomes; limited disclosure of accord provisions makes baseline measurement difficult.

Google Files Two Legal Challenges Against EU Digital Markets Act AI and Search Data Orders

Independent Secondary Reporting The Next Web, Irish Times, Bloomberg · September 29, 2026 · The Next Web · Irish Times · Bloomberg
Platform Governance AI Regulation Data Governance Platform Competition Privacy Digital Markets
Evidence Quality
Independent Secondary Reporting Multiple independent outlets (The Next Web, Irish Times, Bloomberg) corroborate that Google filed two distinct legal challenges at the EU General Court in Luxembourg on September 29, 2026. Quotes from Google's competition director Oliver Bethell and DuckDuckGo's spokesperson are independently reported. The DMA orders themselves (July 2026) are verifiable public EU regulatory actions. Google's privacy argument is a company claim, not an established finding.
Verified Summary

On September 29, 2026, Google filed two lawsuits at the EU General Court in Luxembourg challenging Digital Markets Act compliance orders issued by the European Commission in July 2026. The first order requires Google to provide competing AI assistants with equivalent access to Gemini on 11 Android phone features (including voice activation and in-app actions) by August 2027. The second order requires Google to provide anonymized search data — queries and click-through information — to rival search engines and AI chatbots beginning January 2027.

Google's stated argument: sharing personal search queries "would cause irreversible harm to user privacy" even with anonymization. Oliver Bethell, Google's senior director of competition, issued this statement. The EU Commission stated its orders incorporate anonymization developed with privacy experts and that Google retains authority to deny access to companies posing security or privacy threats. DuckDuckGo publicly supported the EU's position. Under EU law, legal challenges do not automatically suspend compliance deadlines; the 2027 obligations remain in force unless a court intervenes. The Trump administration has previously characterized DMA enforcement as discriminatory toward US companies.

Why This May Matter

This legal challenge represents a high-stakes test of whether existing platform competition regulation can compel AI data sharing — a question with substantial implications for AI ecosystem structure. If upheld, the DMA orders would create mandated interoperability in two dimensions: access to learned model capabilities embedded in Android features, and access to the search behavioral data used to train and improve AI systems. Both create observability into previously proprietary AI infrastructure, with potential downstream effects on AI competitor capabilities.

Google's use of privacy framing as its primary public argument is strategically notable. It may represent a deliberate choice to reframe a market-power dispute as a user protection dispute — a discursive strategy with implications for how regulatory legitimacy is contested. Whether the EU's anonymization approach adequately addresses privacy concerns is technically contestable, but the deployment of privacy as a competitive argument creates an interesting site for studying information asymmetry in regulatory conflicts: the parties with most information about what data sharing would actually entail are the same parties with the strongest interest in the outcome.

For IS researchers, this creates an observable natural experiment: if the DMA orders are ultimately enforced, the subsequent changes in AI competitive dynamics (changes in market share, new entrants using search data, changes in AI assistant quality for non-Google products) become measurable outcomes of a regulatory intervention on platform data access.

Theory Mapping
Two-Sided Market Theory / Network Effects

Core logic: Platforms connecting two or more distinct user groups generate value through network effects; market power arises from multi-homing costs and cross-side network effects (Rochet & Tirole, 2003; Evans, 2003).

Potential fit: The DMA orders attempt to reduce Google's two-sided market advantage by mandating interoperability. Mandated data sharing could reduce the training-data advantage that entrenches Google's AI assistant position. This may provide a theoretically interesting case of regulatory intervention into two-sided market dynamics — specifically, whether mandated data portability can reshape competitive equilibria in AI-mediated search markets.

Key assumptions: Assumes data access is a critical source of AI platform advantage; requires verification that anonymized search data actually materially benefits rivals (an empirically contested claim).

Resource-Based View / Information Asymmetry

Core logic: Competitive advantage derives from rare, inimitable, non-substitutable resources (Barney, 1991); information asymmetry between parties produces strategic behavior and adverse selection (Akerlof; Arrow).

Potential fit: Google's search data is an VRIN resource (valuable, rare, inimitable at scale, non-substitutable) whose forced sharing the DMA effectively mandates. Separately, Google's privacy argument may involve genuine information asymmetry (Google knows more about what its search queries contain than the EU does). Studying how courts adjudicate this asymmetry may reveal how regulatory systems handle contested knowledge claims in algorithmic governance.

Research Opportunity Assessment
Method / Data Opportunity

Research Question: Does mandated AI data interoperability produce measurable increases in market competition in search and AI assistants, and if so, through what mechanisms?

Potential Mechanism: Mandatory search data access lowers training data barriers for rivals, potentially enabling quality improvements in rival search engines and AI assistants. Platform competition theory predicts that reduced data moats would reduce switching costs for users. However, data access alone may be insufficient if rivals lack complementary capabilities in model training, distribution, and brand trust.

Potential Data Source: EU statutory compliance reports; EU market share data (search, AI assistants) before and after 2027 compliance; new product launches by companies using mandated data access; user survey data on AI assistant usage patterns.

Possible Empirical Design: Difference-in-differences comparing AI assistant and search market dynamics in EU (treated) vs. US (control) markets before and after the January 2027 data-sharing deadline — subject to parallel trends assumption.

Main Feasibility Risk: DMA compliance may be delayed further by court proceedings; EU vs. US market comparison requires controlling for many confounds; data on actual market share change is typically available on long lags.

Verified Summary

Unlike standard ChatGPT interactions, Dots operate continuously in background processes, maintaining persistent memory across sessions and integrating with Slack, Microsoft Teams, and other organizational platforms. Why This May Matter

Dots represents a specific and consequential design choice at the product level: anthropomorphic visual identity combined with credential-bearing, cross-platform autonomous authority and an explicit minimal-oversight value proposition. This combination raises a question of potential IS research interest — whether anthropomorphic visual presentation of AI agents (cute "bubble" avatars) affects users' willingness to grant authority, their oversight behavior, and their trust calibration.

The noted parallel with Meta's Muse agent suggests an industry design convergence toward anthropomorphic agentic AI — a pattern that may deserve study as a platform-level design ideology. If multiple major AI platforms independently converge on similar visual and behavioral representations of agents, this may indicate mimetic isomorphism in product design, or it may reflect genuine insights about human acceptance of AI agents. Distinguishing these explanations would require cross-platform and longitudinal research.

The credential provisioning aspect — each Dot receives an "employee ID" with explicit access controls — creates an emerging empirical phenomenon: AI systems that have formally recognized identities within organizational information systems. This may be a precursor to broader organizational identity questions about the status of AI agents in corporate structures.

Theory Mapping
Affordance Theory (Gaver; Gibson; Majchrzak)

Core logic: Affordances are action possibilities that emerge from the relationship between a user's goals and the properties of a technology; different users may perceive different affordances from the same technology.

Potential fit: Dots has multiple affordances whose organizational consequences depend on user perception: the affordance of persistent memory (enabling continuity of complex tasks), the affordance of minimal oversight (enabling but not requiring delegation), and the affordance of visual identity (enabling anthropomorphic interpretation). An affordance theory lens could help explain why the same Dots feature set may lead to very different organizational outcomes depending on user interpretation and organizational context.

Trust Theory (Mayer, Davis & Schoorman; McKnight & Chervany)

Core logic: Trust in a party involves perceptions of ability, benevolence, and integrity; trust formation depends on prior experience, institutional signals, and dispositional tendencies.

Potential fit: Dots' anthropomorphic visual design, credential-based identity, and stated "minimal oversight" philosophy may each affect trust formation differently. Whether visual anthropomorphism increases trust beyond what ability-based trust would warrant is an empirically testable question with governance implications (miscalibrated trust leading to over-delegation).

Cross-Article Synthesis

Emerging Organizational Phenomena

The most prominent organizational pattern across September 29's developments is the emergence of persistent AI agents as organizational participants rather than tools. OpenAI's DevDay positions Dots agents with identity credentials, cross-platform access, and persistent memory — attributes previously reserved for human employees. This structural change in how AI is positioned (from software to participant) is visible in two simultaneous developments: the product design (credentials, persistent identity, "minimal oversight") and the governance response (voluntary accord, Trump framing firms as "policing each other" as if agents in a shared ecosystem). The emerging challenge is that organizational governance frameworks — designed for human actors and software tools — may not map cleanly onto autonomous AI agents that combine properties of both.

Emerging Governance Phenomena

Two distinct governance models are visible in direct juxtaposition today. The White House voluntary accord represents self-regulatory governance: morally framed, institutionally unenforceable, and dependent on industry policing. The EU Digital Markets Act represents regulatory mandate governance: legally enforceable, contested in court, and creating structural market obligations. The co-occurrence of these two events on the same day creates an unusually clean comparative case: the US and EU are simultaneously creating non-comparable governance regimes for the same underlying technology. Whether divergence produces regulatory arbitrage, governance competition, or eventual convergence is an important research question. A related governance tension appears in the Dots product design itself: the explicit "minimal oversight" design philosophy conflicts with the accord's stated commitment to "multiple layers of auditing controls."

Emerging Platform Phenomena

OpenAI's DevDay ecosystem moves (OpenAI Marketplace with 32 vendors, Sign in with ChatGPT for 16 apps, Agents API for third-party development, Bedrock Managed Agents with Amazon) collectively signal an accelerating platform lock-in strategy. Simultaneously, Google's DMA legal challenge demonstrates that incumbent platforms are willing to use courts to resist ecosystem interoperability mandates. Together, these developments suggest AI-mediated platforms are entering a period of aggressive ecosystem boundary-setting — through both voluntary expansion (OpenAI acquiring complementors) and defensive litigation (Google resisting mandated interoperability). Platform governance theory and ecosystem generativity theory are both implicated, though the literature may not have fully theorized the combination of agentic AI intermediaries within platform ecosystems.

Emerging Labor Phenomena

Dots explicitly targets knowledge work tasks: monitoring customer feedback, implementing bug fixes, rerunning scientific analyses. The "employee ID" framing and cross-platform credential provisioning suggest that agentic AI is being positioned not as a productivity tool for workers but as a parallel workforce participant. The labor implications are not yet empirically observable (Dots launched today), but the design framing is consequential: it signals a vendor intent to position AI agents as substitutes for, rather than augmenters of, specific knowledge work roles. This distinction (augmentation vs. substitution) is important for IS research on automation and workforce adaptation.

Emerging Market Phenomena

The OpenAI Marketplace and the DMA search data orders represent opposite directions of the same market phenomenon: AI platform boundaries and the control of access to capabilities and data. OpenAI is voluntarily expanding its marketplace (lowering entry barriers for select complementors while creating financial commitments). Google is legally contesting mandated expansion (resisting required data sharing). In both cases, control of AI-relevant data and capabilities is the central market asset. Whether mandated interoperability can meaningfully reduce incumbent advantages in AI-mediated markets is a testable question enabled by the DMA natural experiment.

Emerging IS Constructs

Several constructs appear repeatedly across today's developments and may warrant conceptual attention: (1) AI agent authority — the formal scope of action granted to an AI agent within organizational information systems, which may require extension of existing IS constructs around authorization and delegation; (2) governance legitimacy gap — the distance between public expectations for AI governance accountability and the institutional mechanisms actually available; (3) anthropomorphic AI trust — trust formation toward AI agents that carry identity markers (avatars, credentials) that trigger social rather than tool-based inference; (4) mandated platform interoperability effectiveness — the actual competitive effects of regulatory access mandates in AI-mediated markets, where data and capability asymmetries are multi-dimensional.

Emerging Theoretical Questions

Theories likely to be useful: Institutional theory (legitimacy, isomorphism, ceremonial adoption) is directly applicable to the voluntary accord; Platform ecosystem theory addresses both the OpenAI Marketplace expansion and the DMA challenge; Agency theory requires extension to handle multi-layer human-AI principal chains; Affordance theory and Trust theory apply to Dots' design implications for user behavior.

Assumptions challenged by today's developments: (1) Agency theory's assumption that agents are human actors with interests — AI agents exhibit goal-directed behavior without interests in the traditional sense, complicating incentive alignment analysis; (2) Platform generativity theory's assumption that platforms voluntarily pursue openness for complementor benefit — mandated interoperability creates a new category of externally-imposed generativity with potentially different dynamics; (3) Institutional theory's assumption that coercive pressure leads to isomorphic structural change — voluntary accord governance with no enforcement mechanism may produce decoupling rather than genuine change, but the "morally binding" framing creates novel normative pressure that prior self-regulatory cases may not have included.

If they do, substantive institutional influence is supported. The "morally binding" language is a theoretically interesting frame — it invokes normative legitimacy while explicitly avoiding legal enforceability.
Relevant Literature Streams: Institutional theory (DiMaggio & Powell; Meyer & Rowan; Suchman); voluntary corporate governance commitments; AI governance literature; corporate social responsibility decoupling research

A3. US-EU AI Governance Divergence: Regulatory Arbitrage or Convergence?

Why Theoretically Interesting: Regulatory divergence between major trading blocs creates a natural experiment for governance theory. Firms operating globally must navigate conflicting requirements. B. Digital Market and Platform Research

B1. Does Mandated AI Data Interoperability Reshape Platform Competition?

Observed Phenomenon: EU DMA orders (resisted by Google in court) would mandate search data sharing with rival search engines and AI chatbots from January 2027.
Why Theoretically Interesting: Two-sided market theory predicts that data access reduces incumbent advantages by lowering complementors' quality disadvantage. AI markets have distinctive features (training data compounding, model-level capabilities) that may make the mechanism different.
Relevant Literature Streams: Platform competition; two-sided markets (Rochet & Tirole; Evans); data portability literature; DMA compliance research; AI market structure research
