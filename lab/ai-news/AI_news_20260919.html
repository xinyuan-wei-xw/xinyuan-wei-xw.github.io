2. Executive Summary

Themes covered in this briefing
  • Recursive AI Self-Involvement in R&D
  • Private AI Governance Institutions
  • Vertical AI Integration in Professional Services
  • AI Infrastructure Regulation and Labor Displacement Concern

September 18, 2026 produced a cluster of developments that collectively illuminate an inflection point in AI’s organizational and institutional trajectory. The most significant single disclosure was Anthropic’s R&D Automation Index, which quantified for the first time that an AI model leads 26 percent of its own development organization’s R&D tasks, up from under 1 percent in eight months, with over 90 percent of work involving AI as a collaborator or better. This recursive self-improvement dynamic raises urgent questions about human-AI coordination, absorptive capacity, and the governance of agents that participate in their own capability development.

Simultaneously, the three largest AI laboratories (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind) announced formal negotiations toward a FINRA-style self-regulatory standards body, marking a shift from voluntary safety commitments to institutionalized pre-release testing. This development represents a nascent private governance infrastructure with profound implications for platform ecosystem theory, institutional legitimacy, and antitrust dynamics, particularly given smaller firms’ immediate cartel accusations.

At the sectoral level, Novo Nordisk’s adoption of Claude for drug discovery and OpenAI’s launch of Astra for Law signal the transition from horizontal AI deployment to vertical workflow integration, with implications for professional knowledge work, task-technology fit, and human capital reconfiguration. China’s Z.ai deploying a frontier model on 100,000 domestic chips at Nvidia-comparable cost challenges assumptions about export control efficacy. Domestically, a Pew survey documented a 17-point surge in Democratic concern about AI job displacement, while the US House passed the Ratepayer Protection Act 417-3, redirecting AI infrastructure costs from residential consumers to data centers. Together, these developments reveal emerging tensions between recursive AI capability growth, fragmented private governance, accelerating professional-sector adoption, and growing societal anxiety about labor displacement.

3. Key Takeaways

1

AI systems are becoming embedded participants in their own development cycles, creating recursive capability dynamics that existing organizational learning and human-AI collaboration theories are not equipped to explain.

2

Major AI laboratories are shifting from unilateral safety pledges to collaborative private governance institutions modeled on financial industry self-regulation, generating both legitimacy and antitrust risk.

3

Vertical AI integration in professional services (law, pharmaceuticals) is advancing faster than horizontal enterprise adoption, reshaping task structures, expertise hierarchies, and the economics of professional knowledge work.

4

Public concern about AI-driven job displacement has reached a tipping point, surpassing concern about AI safety risks and beginning to align along partisan lines, which will shape regulatory politics and enterprise adoption narratives.

5

Domestic chip parity claims from China, if validated, signal that export control regimes may be insufficient to manage AI capability diffusion, necessitating new frameworks for understanding global AI ecosystem competition and institutional responses.

Verified Summary

Anthropic published the first results of a prototype R&D Automation Index reporting that Claude leads 26 percent of the company’s AI R&D work as of August 2026, up from under 1 percent in February. The index uses Epoch AI’s Automation Level scale (AL0 to AL5) applied to approximately 15,000 granular tasks sampled from 20 percent of staff and organized into a 542-node hierarchy. More than 90 percent of tasks now involve Claude at the collaborator level or above. Approximately 30,000 research and engineering agents operate simultaneously, with monitors blocking 1 in 47,000 actions before execution and flagging roughly 100,000 transcripts per week for offline review, of which about 50 escalate to humans.

Why This Matters

This is the first empirical quantification of recursive AI self-involvement: a model actively participating in its own development cycle at scale. For organizations, it transforms AI from a tool to an organizational agent with a growing share of productive output, challenging traditional notions of organizational hierarchy and knowledge authority. For markets, it signals that the production frontier of AI capabilities may shift faster than competitive dynamics assume, compressing the strategic planning horizons of firms that rely on capability gaps. For workers, especially knowledge workers in R&D roles, the 26 percent leadership figure represents a concrete displacement metric rather than a speculative forecast. For regulators, the disclosure of safety compute at 6 percent of R&D compute provides a benchmark against which future auditing frameworks might be calibrated, though Anthropic explicitly cautions this is a one-week snapshot.

Construct Mapping

Task Automation

Definition: The degree to which AI agents independently complete defined R&D subtasks without human initiation or correction. Typical Measures: Automation Level (AL0-AL5) per task category, weighted by person-time. Empirical Operationalization: Percentage of tasks at AL3+ in an organization’s documented workflow inventory.

Human-AI Coordination

Definition: The mechanisms through which human and AI agents coordinate on joint tasks, including escalation, monitoring, and override structures. Typical Measures: Escalation rate, override rate, human-initiated versus AI-initiated task transitions. Empirical Operationalization: Agent log analysis: ratio of autonomous completions to escalations; time-to-escalation distributions.

Absorptive Capacity

Definition: An organization’s ability to recognize, assimilate, and apply AI-generated knowledge to its own capability development. Typical Measures: Share of AI-generated research outputs incorporated into model training; researcher citations to AI-produced work. Empirical Operationalization: Content analysis of training dataset provenance combined with researcher survey data.

Algorithmic Control

Definition: The extent to which organizational processes are governed by automated rule-based systems rather than human discretion. Typical Measures: Block rate, flag rate, escalation rate of automated safety monitors. Empirical Operationalization: Monitor action logs: proportion of decisions made algorithmically vs. human review.

Theory Mapping

Adaptive Structuration Theory (AST)

Introduction: AST (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994) holds that advanced information technologies provide structural features that groups appropriate and adapt through their actual use, producing social structures that may diverge from those designed. The theory emphasizes the recursive interplay between technology, social interaction, and emergent structures.

Core Logic: AI agent features (automation levels, monitoring thresholds) are appropriated by research teams in ways that produce new divisions of labor and workflow norms not anticipated in system design.

Fit to Article: As Claude’s AL level rises, research teams adapt their coordination practices in ways that feed back into the system’s design priorities. The recursive element — Claude participating in its own structuration — extends AST into self-referential territory.

References: DeSanctis, G., & Poole, M.S. (1994). Capturing the complexity in advanced technology use: Adaptive structuration theory. Organization Science, 5(2), 121-147.

Organizational Learning Theory

Introduction: Organizational learning theory (Levitt & March, 1988; Argote, 1999) explains how organizations accumulate knowledge through experience, encode it in routines, and transfer it across units and time. March’s (1991) exploration-exploitation framework distinguishes between learning novel capabilities and refining existing ones.

Core Logic: When AI agents lead 26 percent of R&D tasks, the locus of organizational learning shifts: experience accumulates in model weights rather than exclusively in human cognition, complicating theories of knowledge retention and organizational memory.

Fit to Article: If Claude leads a quarter of Anthropic’s R&D, organizational memory increasingly resides in model parameters trained on that work. Standard measures of organizational learning (experience curves, knowledge transfer metrics) do not capture this form of distributed encoding.

References: Levitt, B., & March, J.G. (1988). Organizational learning. Annual Review of Sociology, 14, 319-340. March, J.G. (1991). Exploration and exploitation in organizational learning. Organization Science, 2(1), 71-87.

Sociotechnical Systems Theory

Introduction: Sociotechnical systems theory (Trist & Bamforth, 1951; Mumford, 2006) argues that technological and social subsystems are interdependent and must be jointly optimized. It emphasizes that technological change cannot be understood apart from the social relations and organizational structures it co-evolves with.

Core Logic: Recursive AI involvement in R&D is not merely a technical change but a sociotechnical reconfiguration of who (or what) constitutes a knowledge worker, how expertise is certified, and how organizational identity and authority are maintained.

Fit to Article: The Automation Index reveals a sociotechnical system in which the technical subsystem (AI agents) is actively reshaping the social subsystem (researcher roles and tasks) in real time, creating dynamic optimization challenges for organizational design.

References: Trist, E.L., & Bamforth, K.W. (1951). Some social and psychological consequences of the longwall method of coal-getting. Human Relations, 4(1), 3-38. Mumford, E. (2006). The story of socio-technical design. Information Systems Journal, 16(4), 317-338.

OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind are developing a self-regulatory standards body modeled on FINRA (Financial Industry Regulatory Authority) to test powerful AI systems before release. OpenAI global policy chief Chris Lehane confirmed the three largest labs have been coordinating for weeks. The initiative, originally proposed by Google DeepMind’s Demis Hassabis in July 2026, would be industry-funded with third-party evaluators embedded in member organizations. Cohere CEO Aidan Gomez publicly accused the three of forming a cartel, arguing the governance of who controls the rules serves incumbents’ interests. Why This Matters

This is the most consequential governance development of 2026 to date. The shift from voluntary lab pledges to an institutionalized pre-release testing body creates a new layer of private ordering that will govern frontier AI access, deployment, and competition. For organizations beyond the three founding labs, the standards body creates an external legitimacy hurdle: models that fail pre-release tests may not reach market, concentrating platform power among actors who shape the testing criteria. For platforms and developers, the body defines a new form of ecosystem governance analogous to app store review, but applied to AI models themselves. For regulators, the FINRA analogy is instructive: FINRA’s authority derives from an SEC mandate; without legislative underpinning, the AI version risks fragmentation, regulatory arbitrage, or eventual supersession.

Theory Mapping

Institutional Theory (Isomorphism)

Introduction: Institutional theory (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983; Scott, 2008) explains how organizations adopt similar structures and practices in response to coercive, mimetic, and normative pressures from their institutional environment. Organizations seeking legitimacy conform to institutionalized templates even when such conformity does not improve efficiency.

Core Logic: The FINRA-style body creates a new institutional field with coercive pressure (pre-release testing as a de facto requirement for market entry), normative pressure (industry best practices codified by incumbents), and mimetic pressure (smaller labs adopting the evaluation frameworks the body produces).

Fit to Article: Smaller AI labs and enterprise AI adopters will face mounting institutional pressure to align with the standards body’s framework, potentially reinforcing incumbent advantage independent of actual technical merit. The body itself may be best understood as a legitimacy-seeking mechanism.

References: DiMaggio, P.J., & Powell, W.W. (1983). The iron cage revisited: Institutional isomorphism and collective rationality in organizational fields. American Sociological Review, 48(2), 147-160.

Platform Ecosystem Theory

Introduction: Platform ecosystem theory (Parker, Van Alstyne & Choudary, 2016; Eisenmann, Parker & Van Alstyne, 2006) examines how platform owners govern two-sided and multi-sided markets, manage access, set quality controls, and extract value from complementors. Governance rules define who can participate and on what terms.

Core Logic: The standards body functions as a meta-governance layer above individual platforms, creating new entry barriers and determining which models are eligible to become platform infrastructure for developers and enterprises.

Fit to Article: If the standards body operates analogously to an app store review process applied to AI models, theories of platform governance predict that incumbents who control the review criteria will gain disproportionate ecosystem power over complementors.

References: Parker, G., Van Alstyne, M., & Choudary, S.P. (2016). Platform Revolution. W.W. Norton. Eisenmann, T., Parker, G., & Van Alstyne, M.W. (2006). Strategies for two-sided markets. Harvard Business Review, 84(10), 92.

Theory Mapping

Task-Technology Fit Theory

Introduction: Task-Technology Fit (TTF) theory (Goodhue & Thompson, 1995) proposes that the match between task requirements and technology capabilities determines whether a technology positively impacts individual performance. Technologies are more likely to be adopted and to enhance performance when they fit the demands of the tasks they support.

Core Logic: Vertically-tuned AI products are explicitly designed to improve task-technology fit for domain-specific workflows, in contrast to general-purpose models that require users to bridge capability gaps themselves.

Fit to Article: Legal tasks have well-defined quality criteria (accuracy of case citation, coherence of argument structure), enabling objective TTF measurement. Astra for Law’s domain fine-tuning is a direct manipulation of the task-technology fit construct.

References: Goodhue, D.L., & Thompson, R.L. (1995). Task-technology fit and individual performance. MIS Quarterly, 19(2), 213-236.

Generativity Theory

Introduction: Generativity theory (Zittrain, 2006; Boudreau & Lakhani, 2009) explains how platform technologies enable third-party innovation by providing accessible interfaces. When platforms enter markets served by complementors, they face a fundamental tension between generativity and control.

Core Logic: OpenAI launching Astra for Law competes directly with legal AI intermediaries built on its API, reducing the generativity value proposition for the legal vertical and potentially discouraging future complementor investment.

Fit to Article: The Astra for Law launch is a canonical case of platform encroachment on a generative layer, with measurable effects on complementor behavior, developer community sentiment, and the scope of third-party legal AI development.

References: Zittrain, J.L. (2006). The generative internet. Harvard Law Review, 119(7), 1974-2040. Boudreau, K., & Lakhani, K. (2009). How to manage outside innovation. MIT Sloan Management Review, 50(4), 69-76.

Theory Mapping

Absorptive Capacity

Introduction: Absorptive capacity (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Zahra & George, 2002) refers to a firm’s ability to recognize the value of new external knowledge, assimilate it, and apply it to commercial ends. It is built through prior related knowledge and shaped by organizational routines that process external information.

Core Logic: A firm’s capacity to effectively integrate AI models into R&D workflows depends on its existing computational biology expertise, data infrastructure, and organizational routines for iterating on research hypotheses — its prior absorptive capacity.

Fit to Article: Novo Nordisk’s ability to realize value from Claude in drug discovery is predicted by its prior investment in computational biology, data science talent, and digital R&D infrastructure. Firms with higher prior absorptive capacity should achieve faster and deeper AI assimilation in professional workflows.

References: Cohen, W.M., & Levinthal, D.A. (1990). Absorptive capacity: A new perspective on learning and innovation. Administrative Science Quarterly, 35(1), 128-152.

Technology Assimilation Theory

Introduction: Technology assimilation research (Fichman & Kemerer, 1999; Zhu et al., 2006) distinguishes between adoption (initial deployment) and assimilation (deep use that produces organizational outcomes). Many enterprise technologies are adopted but not assimilated, with post-adoption use determining value.

Core Logic: The announcement marks adoption, but value realization depends on assimilation: integration into research workflows, training of scientists to prompt effectively, and embedding AI outputs into decision-making pipelines.

Fit to Article: Novo Nordisk’s CEO statement that AI will ‘supercharge’ R&D sets a high assimilation expectation. Longitudinal measurement of how deeply Claude is integrated into hypothesis generation, compound screening, and regulatory documentation workflows would distinguish adoption from assimilation.

References: Fichman, R.G., & Kemerer, C.F. (1999). The illusory diffusion of innovation. Information Systems Research, 10(3), 255-275. Zhu, K., Dong, S., Xu, S.X., & Kraemer, K.L. (2006). Innovation diffusion in global contexts. Journal of the Association for Information Systems, 7(8), 491-516.

Theory Mapping

Algorithmic Aversion and Appreciation

Introduction: Research on algorithmic aversion (Dietvorst et al., 2015) and algorithmic appreciation (Logg et al., 2019) examines conditions under which individuals resist or prefer algorithmic outputs over human judgment. These opposing tendencies are moderated by task domain, outcome visibility, and prior experience with algorithms.

Core Logic: When AI-driven job displacement becomes empirically visible (through published productivity data and public layoff announcements), algorithmic aversion may shift from outcome-based (I distrust AI outputs) to threat-based (I fear AI consequences), motivating different behavioral and political responses.

Fit to Article: The Pew finding’s 17-point jump in job-loss concern, coinciding with publication of Anthropic’s R&D index and developer productivity data, suggests a threshold effect in algorithmic threat perception that could explain the partisan realignment. IS research can leverage this natural experiment.

References: Dietvorst, B.J., Logg, J.M., & Logg, J.M. (2015). Algorithm aversion. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 144(1), 114-126. Logg, J.M., Minson, J.A., & Moore, D.A. (2019). Algorithm appreciation. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 151, 90-103.

Institutional Theory

Introduction: Institutional theory (Scott, 2008; Suchman, 1995) explains how organizations and technologies gain or lose societal legitimacy through conformity to social norms, regulatory requirements, and cultural expectations. Legitimacy crises can trigger regulatory responses and adoption slowdowns.

Introduction: Coercive isomorphism (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983) occurs when organizations adopt practices in response to legal mandates or strong expectations from bodies on which they depend. Core Logic: The Ratepayer Protection Act creates a new cost burden for data center operators, forcing cost internalization that may make some projects economically unviable and redirecting investment toward jurisdictions with more favorable regulatory treatment.

Transaction Cost Economics

Introduction: Transaction cost economics (Williamson, 1985) explains organizational boundary decisions based on asset specificity, uncertainty, and frequency of transactions. Location decisions for data centers involve high asset specificity (physical infrastructure), uncertainty (regulatory risk), and recurrent transactions (power procurement).

Core Logic: The Ratepayer Protection Act increases the transaction costs of locating data centers in jurisdictions with constrained grid infrastructure by imposing new cost obligations. Firms will respond by preferring locations with surplus grid capacity or by vertically integrating into power generation (as Amazon’s Generac deal illustrates).

Fit to Article: The bill’s cost internalization mechanism is analytically equivalent to increasing the transaction cost of grid connection services for data center operators. TCE predicts vertical integration (own generation) or governance mechanisms that reduce exposure (long-term power purchase agreements with generators).

References: Williamson, O.E. (1985). The Economic Institutions of Capitalism. Free Press.

Signaling Theory

Introduction: Signaling theory (Spence, 1973) explains how individuals and organizations invest in observable signals to convey private information to less-informed parties in markets characterized by information asymmetry. Effective signals must be costly to produce but differentially so for high- versus low-quality types.

Core Logic: The MCP Associate certification creates a formal signal for AI agent developer competence in a market where such competence is otherwise unobservable. Employers, clients, and platform orchestrators can use certification status as a screening mechanism.

Fit to Article: In a labor market for agentic AI developers where skills are novel and difficult to verify through traditional indicators (degrees, prior job titles), a vendor-neutral certification that requires demonstrated competence fills an information asymmetry gap that would otherwise slow adoption.

References: Spence, A.M. (1973). Job market signaling. Quarterly Journal of Economics, 87(3), 355-374. Connelly, B.L., Certo, S.T., Ireland, R.D., & Reutzel, C.R. (2011). Signaling theory. Journal of Management, 37(1), 39-67.

Commons Governance Theory

Introduction: Commons governance theory (Ostrom, 1990) analyzes how communities develop institutional arrangements to govern shared resources and prevent collective action problems. Online developer communities and open technical standards share properties with commons resources.

Core Logic: MCP as an open protocol creates a commons around agentic AI interoperability. The certification provides governance infrastructure to ensure competent stewardship of this commons, reducing the risk of fragmentation, incompatible implementations, and security vulnerabilities.

Fit to Article: The MCP Associate certification can be understood as an institutional governance mechanism analogous to Ostrom’s design principles: it defines boundaries of legitimate practice, establishes graduated sanctions for incompetent implementation, and creates recognition for collective management of the MCP commons.

References: Ostrom, E. (1990). Governing the Commons. Cambridge University Press.

5. Cross-Article Synthesis

Emerging Organizational Phenomena

The most striking organizational pattern across September 18’s news cycle is the recursive embedding of AI agents in organizational productive processes. Anthropic’s R&D index shows AI accounting for 26 percent of R&D leadership within a single organization, Novo Nordisk integrating AI into drug discovery workflows, and OpenAI deploying a vertically specialized agent for legal work. Organizations are no longer using AI as an isolated productivity tool — they are restructuring workflows around agents that perform core professional tasks and, in Anthropic’s case, participate in creating the next generation of the same technology. This suggests a new organizational form is emerging: the AI-augmented R&D organization, in which human and AI roles are dynamically redistributed at a pace that outstrips traditional change management frameworks.

Emerging Governance Phenomena

Three distinct governance phenomena converged on September 18. First, private self-regulatory governance is being institutionalized at the frontier AI level (FINRA-style standards body). Second, infrastructure governance is being re-regulated at the grid level (Ratepayer Protection Act). Third, professional practice governance is being developed at the practitioner level (MCP Associate certification). These three layers — frontier capability, infrastructure, and professional practice — represent a nascent, fragmented governance stack for AI deployment. No single legislative or regulatory framework coordinates across all three levels, creating significant gaps and potential inconsistencies that will generate both compliance burden for firms and research opportunities for governance scholars.

Emerging Platform Phenomena

OpenAI’s launch of Astra for Law represents the clearest platform-level shift of the day: a foundation model provider entering the vertical application market that complementors built on its API. This encroachment dynamic, combined with the FINRA-style body that would give the three largest labs governance authority over who can participate in the AI ecosystem, signals a consolidation of platform power at the model layer. At the same time, Z.ai’s claim of Nvidia-comparable performance on domestic Chinese chips challenges the technological lock-in that has supported current platform power hierarchies. If validated, chip parity expands the competitive frontier and creates alternative platform trajectories in markets where export controls restrict access to Nvidia.

Emerging Labor Phenomena

The Pew survey’s 17-point jump in Democratic AI job-loss concern, occurring in the same news cycle as Anthropic’s disclosure that Claude leads 26 percent of R&D work, is not coincidental: public attitudes appear to be updating on concrete productivity data. Knowledge workers in professional services (law, pharmaceutical R&D) are the first to experience task-level AI substitution in observable ways, and the professional vertical product launches (Astra for Law, Novo Nordisk’s Claude adoption) confirm that this substitution is accelerating. The MCP certification simultaneously creates new roles for AI practitioners, suggesting a dual labor market dynamic: incumbent professional workers facing substitution risk and emerging AI specialist roles commanding scarcity premiums.

Emerging IS Constructs
  • AI Automation Level (the Epoch AI AL0-AL5 scale, now operationalized and published by a major lab)
  • Recursive AI Involvement (a new construct capturing AI’s participation in its own development cycle, not captured by prior technology assimilation constructs)
  • Agentic Workflow Integration (the depth to which AI agents are embedded in core productive processes rather than used as peripheral productivity tools)
  • Private Governance Legitimacy (the institutional legitimacy of industry-funded self-regulatory bodies for AI, analogous to but distinct from financial industry self-regulation)
  • Algorithmic Job Threat Perception (a more specific and empirically grounded construct than general algorithmic aversion, tied to observable labor market data)
Emerging Theoretical Opportunities

Adaptive Structuration Theory extended to recursive AI participation appears most promising, as it can capture both the structuring effects of AI features and the novel element of AI contributing to the redesign of those features. Institutional Theory proves broadly applicable across all governance-related articles, particularly around legitimacy dynamics of private AI governance. Absorptive Capacity remains highly relevant for understanding differential enterprise AI assimilation across the pharmaceutical, legal, and technology sectors. Platform Ecosystem Theory extended to meta-governance layers (standards bodies governing platforms governing developers) captures the nested governance structures emerging in the AI ecosystem. The most theoretically underserved phenomenon is recursive AI self-improvement: no established IS theory adequately addresses a technology that participates in designing its successors.

7. Most Promising Research Opportunity

Selected Opportunity: The Recursive Capability Trap — AI Self-Involvement, Organizational Knowledge, and Governance Design
Why It Is ImportantAnthropic’s R&D Automation Index represents the most consequential empirical disclosure in the history of AI adoption research: a major laboratory publicly quantified that an AI model leads over a quarter of the work that produces the next version of the same model. This is not AI as a productivity tool applied to standard business processes. This is AI as a participant in its own evolution, operating at organizational scale, in real time, with measurable task coverage. The phenomenon — which we term Recursive AI Involvement — is categorically different from any prior technology adoption event because the technology being adopted is the same technology being developed. No prior IS theory was built to explain this configuration, and no empirical study has measured it. Why Now Is the Right TimeAnthropic’s disclosure creates a foundational measurement artifact — the R&D Automation Index — that is likely to be adopted by other organizations as a governance and reporting standard, particularly if the proposed FINRA-style standards body (also announced September 18) makes such indices a disclosure requirement. The current moment is the equivalent of the early diffusion period for ERP systems or cloud computing: the phenomenon exists at one visible organization, the measurement framework has just been published, and the window for IS scholars to establish construct ownership, develop validated instruments, and publish foundational empirical work is precisely now. Within eighteen months, the phenomenon will be widespread enough to have established measurement norms that IS scholars did not shape. Why IS Scholars Have Comparative AdvantageInformation Systems scholars bring a unique combination of organizational theory depth (missing from computer science approaches), technology artifact understanding (missing from pure organizational behavior approaches), and empirical design expertise suited to longitudinal multi-level data. The IS literature on absorptive capacity, technology assimilation, and human-AI collaboration provides the theoretical vocabulary for studying recursive AI involvement. IS scholars also have established relationships with enterprise technology providers and are positioned to negotiate research access to the kinds of agent logs and workflow data that would enable direct measurement of automation levels within organizations. Why ISR, MISQ, or Management Science Would CareISR and MISQ would care because Recursive AI Involvement is a fundamentally new IS phenomenon that challenges core assumptions in the human-AI collaboration literature: that humans are the principals and AI systems are the agents, that organizational knowledge resides primarily in human cognition and routines, and that technology assimilation follows a path from adoption to institutionalization driven by human decisions. The recursive case inverts or complicates all three assumptions. Management Science would care because the phenomenon has direct implications for innovation economics: if AI agents contribute to their own capability improvements, the production function for AI capabilities changes in ways that affect forecasting, investment, and market structure analysis. Constructs and Theories Most PromisingThe three most theoretically promising constructs are: (1) Recursive AI Involvement — the percentage of an organization’s productive tasks in which AI agents participate at AL3 or above, specifically applied to tasks that contribute to AI capability development; (2) Epistemic Governance — the mechanisms organizations use to maintain knowledge integrity and quality standards when AI agents are both producing and evaluating knowledge; and (3) Organizational Memory Distribution — the degree to which organizational knowledge is encoded in AI model parameters versus human cognition and formal documentation. The most applicable theories are Adaptive Structuration Theory (for the recursive structuration dynamic), Organizational Learning Theory (for the knowledge encoding and memory implications), and Sociotechnical Systems Theory (for the joint optimization challenge when the technical subsystem participates in its own design). The governance literature on commons management (Ostrom) is also relevant for the agent monitoring and escalation systems Anthropic describes. Most Feasible Empirical SettingThe most immediately feasible empirical setting is a multi-wave survey and interview study of organizations that have publicly announced agentic AI deployments in R&D or knowledge production roles. The Anthropic R&D index provides a template measurement instrument that researchers can adapt for other organizations. Collaborating with an enterprise agentic AI platform provider (e.g., Cohere Command R+, Microsoft Copilot for R&D, Salesforce Agentforce) to access anonymized agent log data would enable the quantitative component. The qualitative component would involve longitudinal interviews with researchers and managers at three to five organizations at different stages of AI automation, tracking how their descriptions of knowledge authority, task ownership, and organizational memory change as automation levels rise. A five-year longitudinal design would capture at least two cycles of the automation index within each organization, enabling growth curve modeling of how recursive AI involvement evolves and what governance mechanisms moderate its effects on knowledge integrity and organizational performance.
